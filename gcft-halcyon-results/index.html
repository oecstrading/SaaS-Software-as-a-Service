0:00–0:25

GCFT: from climate commitments to accountable results

A Theory of Change for Small Island Developing States

  • B. Cuthbert John, PMP
  • OECS AI Institute
  • Halcyon discussion, 19 November 2026
Speaker notes

Good morning. I am B. Cuthbert John. GCFT addresses a practical question: how can a small island state show what its climate spending is intended to achieve, what actually happened, and who benefited? Our proposed answer is a shared accountability platform. I will explain the problem, the solution and the Theory of Change we will use to test whether it works.

0:25–1:20

The gap is between climate commitments and usable evidence

Fragmented records make climate-finance decisions harder

  • Climate plans and budgets sit in separate systems.
  • Project evidence and financial records do not connect consistently.
  • Institutions repeatedly reconstruct reports and resolve evidence gaps.
  • Affected communities need visibility into intended benefits.
Speaker notes

Small island states face climate risks while operating with limited institutional and fiscal capacity. Our starting diagnosis is that climate commitments, budgets, project records and reporting obligations often sit in separate spreadsheets and documents. A reviewer may see a climate label without finding the climate rationale, supporting expenditure or beneficiary evidence. Staff then reconstruct information for each reporting request. This can weaken readiness and oversight. It may contribute to delays, but finance constraints also involve project quality, institutional decisions and funder requirements. We therefore treat the information gap as one solvable constraint, rather than claiming software alone can solve climate finance. Our first pilot must establish how serious that gap is for a specific institution.

1:20–2:05

GCFT connects budgets, projects and reviewable evidence

Gender and Climate Finance Tagging with MRV-as-a-Service

  • Classify climate and gender relevance with human approval.
  • Link expenditure, indicators and source documents to each project.
  • Track evidence gaps, corrective actions and review history.
  • Reuse approved records in dashboards and funder-oriented reports.
Speaker notes

GCFT is designed to connect the information that reviewers need. A budget or project enters a structured record. AI can suggest climate and gender classifications, while an authorized person reviews the rationale. Evidence then links expenditure to implementation milestones and results indicators. Missing information becomes a task with an owner, rather than disappearing inside a spreadsheet. Reviewed records can support several reports without rebuilding the underlying evidence each time. MRV means measurement, reporting and verification. The platform organizes that process, while qualified institutions retain responsibility for verification and funding decisions. These are intended capabilities from our Theory of Change, subject to implementation testing.

2:05–3:15

Our Theory of Change makes the causal steps explicit

Better records must change institutional decisions to create impact

  • INPUTS
    Software, methods and partners
  • ACTIVITIES
    Tag, collect, review and train
  • OUTPUTS
    Reviewed records and reports
  • OUTCOMES
    Readiness and oversight
  • IMPACT
    Accountable finance and resilience
Speaker notes

Our Theory of Change separates what we provide from what we hope will change. Inputs include software, methodologies, institutional staff, partnerships and training. Activities include tagging budgets, collecting evidence, reviewing quality and preparing reports. Immediate outputs are reviewed records, evidence registers and traceable reporting packages. Short-term outcomes should include more complete data and less time reconstructing reports. Medium-term outcomes may include more credible project preparation and better fiduciary oversight. The long-term contribution is better-managed climate finance and more inclusive resilience. Every link depends on assumptions. Staff must use the workflow, source data must be credible, and funders must find the information useful. Climate projects must then deliver their intended results. We will test these links and revise the approach where evidence contradicts our assumptions.

3:15–4:05

One project shows how the framework works

Illustrative coastal cold-storage resilience project

  • WHY
    Heat and disruptions threaten fishers' cold chain
  • WHAT
    Record a proposed resilient cold-storage investment
  • EVIDENCE
    Link costs, temperature records and maintenance logs
  • REVIEW
    Check climate rationale, safeguards and gender actions
  • RESULT
    Track service continuity and who actually benefits
Speaker notes

Consider an illustrative cold-storage project serving a coastal fishing community. The project owner first documents the climate hazard and why the proposed investment addresses it. GCFT records the budget, responsible organization and expected results. During implementation, invoices, equipment records, temperature measurements and maintenance logs provide evidence. Reviewers check the safeguards and whether the gender action plan addresses actual barriers, such as who can access storage services and at what cost. The outcome is not the number of documents uploaded. It is whether the service performs more reliably and benefits reach the intended people. The physical investment creates those benefits. GCFT makes the commitments and supporting evidence easier to examine.

4:05–4:55

ESG turns into responsibilities and evidence

Environmental, Social and Governance dimensions

  • E
    ENVIRONMENTAL · Climate rationale, environmental risks and results evidence
  • S
    SOCIAL · Gender assessment, equitable access and stakeholder feedback
  • G
    GOVERNANCE · Named reviewers, controlled access and accountable decisions
Speaker notes

ESG stands for Environmental, Social and Governance. For the environmental dimension, GCFT should connect the climate rationale to documented risks, mitigation measures and results. For the social dimension, it should capture gender assessments, participation, access barriers and disaggregated benefits. For governance, it should record who made a decision, on what evidence and under which authority. Data ownership and access rules matter because public financial information can be sensitive. These functions can support ESG assessment and disclosure. They do not create a universal ESG certification, replace environmental and social due diligence, or prove that a project is compliant. The applicable standard and responsible reviewer must remain explicit.

4:55–6:00

GCFT supports preparation for GCF assessment

The Green Climate Fund retains its own appraisal and approval process

  • Impact potential
    Climate rationale and results evidence
  • Paradigm-shift potential
    Replication and institutional learning
  • Sustainable development
    Documented co-benefits and gender actions
  • Needs of the recipient
    Vulnerability and financing constraints
  • Country ownership
    National priorities and NDA coordination
  • Efficiency and effectiveness
    Costs, alternatives and financial controls
Speaker notes

The Green Climate Fund evaluates proposals against six investment criteria. GCFT can help organize the evidence for each: climate impact, potential for wider change, sustainable-development benefits, recipient needs, country ownership, and efficiency and effectiveness. For example, a country-ownership record can link a project to national priorities and the responsible authority. Gender assessments and action plans require substantive analysis, indicators, responsibilities and resources. A checklist alone is insufficient. Institutions use an applicable access route, such as an Accredited Entity or the project-specific assessment pathway, with national endorsement requirements. GCFT is proposed supporting infrastructure in that process. It is not a GCF accreditation, an approved funding channel or a guarantee of financing. Our goal is to improve the quality and traceability of preparation and implementation evidence.

Priority contributions, with project-level evidence

    CLIMATE ACTION · Planning, institutional capacity and resilience evidence

  • 16
    ACCOUNTABLE INSTITUTIONS · Transparent reviews and traceable public decisions
  • 17

Speaker notes

Our clearest SDG connections are Goals thirteen, five, sixteen and seventeen. Goal thirteen concerns climate action and institutional capacity. Goal five supports gender equality, including systems for tracking gender-responsive allocations. Goal sixteen concerns effective and accountable institutions. Goal seventeen emphasizes partnerships and better data. These links should guide project design and measurement. We will not claim that producing a dashboard achieves an SDG. We must show the relevant institutional change and, where possible, the beneficiary outcome. Sector-specific projects may also contribute to energy, oceans or other goals, but those claims need their own evidence.

6:45–7:40

The pilot tests outcomes as well as outputs

Proposed targets, subject to partner agreement and baseline

  • Data quality
    At least 95% mandatory metadata complete
  • Human accountability
    100% AI classifications receive authorized review
  • Efficiency
    Test a 30% reduction in matched-task preparation time
  • Adoption
    Document repeated use and a continuation decision
  • Longer-term contribution
    Track finance and beneficiary outcomes separately
Speaker notes

The pilot must test whether the platform changes work, not simply count uploads. We propose measuring mandatory-field completeness, the proportion of AI classifications reviewed, preparation time and repeated use. A provisional efficiency target is a thirty-percent reduction in preparation time for matched reporting tasks. That is a proposed test, not a result or promise. We will first measure the baseline and record the limits of the comparison. An independent domain reviewer should sample classifications and check the evidence. Later, we can track projects progressing through financing stages and document beneficiary outcomes. We should distinguish finance recorded in the system, finance approved by institutions and finance actually disbursed. None should automatically be attributed to GCFT.

7:40–8:25

A focused service pilot reduces adoption risk

One institutional payer and one defined reporting workflow

  • Begin with a lender, implementing partner or public institution.
  • Agree data rights, scope, responsibilities and acceptance criteria.
  • Deliver onboarding and reporting support through MRVaaS.
  • Expand only after evidence of useful outcomes and willingness to pay.
Speaker notes

Our earlier government-first sales effort has not produced a repeatable commercial channel. That experience changes our first adoption step. We propose one institutional payer with an immediate reporting need, potentially a lender or implementing partner that already manages a climate portfolio. Government ownership and national alignment remain central where public data or GCF activity is involved. The first offer combines onboarding, methodology mapping and reporting support with platform access. Pricing and service costs need validation before expansion. A single workflow gives us a manageable test of user demand, data governance and delivery cost. It also avoids relying on a nationwide procurement decision before learning whether the service creates value.

8:25–9:00

The next step is a jointly designed validation pilot

Three decisions for Halcyon and potential partners

  • Who has the reporting problem and authority to sponsor a pilot?
  • Who will independently challenge the methods and evidence?
  • What result would justify adoption, redesign or stopping?
Speaker notes

I am asking for help identifying a pilot sponsor and an independent climate-finance reviewer. Together, we can select the reporting workflow, agree data rights and cost a limited pilot. We can then decide what evidence would justify adoption, redesign or stopping. The Theory of Change is our discipline for that decision. GCFT aims to help island institutions connect climate commitments with reviewable evidence and accountable action. I welcome your questions, especially where you see an assumption that needs a stronger test.

Questions and discussion

1. Why is this needed if governments already have financial systems?

Public financial systems record transactions. Our proposed value is the connection between a climate classification, the project rationale, implementation evidence and results. We would map the existing systems first and test whether GCFT reduces duplication. Where an existing system already meets the need, integration or a narrower service is preferable.

2. Why have governments not bought it, and who will pay now?

The government-first sales effort has not produced a repeatable channel. We propose a limited service pilot with one institution that already has an evidence or reporting obligation, such as a lender or implementing partner. Government can still be a user or owner of public data. A paid agreement and a costed scope must test the buyer hypothesis.

3. How does the Theory of Change prove that software creates impact?

It does not prove the result in advance. It makes each causal step and assumption testable. We can directly measure data completeness, review quality, time saved and adoption. Finance decisions and resilience outcomes occur later and depend on other actors. We would report contribution and competing explanations rather than attribute every result to GCFT.

4. Is GCFT approved by GCF or does it guarantee funding?

No such approval is claimed. GCFT can organize evidence for country priorities, appraisal and reporting. The applicant must use the applicable GCF access route and satisfy national endorsement and funder requirements. AEs or eligible project-specific applicants and GCF retain their responsibilities. Using our platform does not create eligibility or approval.

5. What is working today, and what remains to be demonstrated?

I would distinguish completed workflows from planned integrations during a controlled demonstration. The TOC describes the intended system; it is not independent production assurance. Before handling sensitive institutional data, we need module tests, methodology review, access-control checks and an agreed operational owner. The first pilot will test specific workflows against acceptance criteria.

6. How will you prevent AI from misclassifying expenditure?

We propose methodology-specific, versioned rules, evidence requirements and authorized human approval. Reviewers should see the rationale and source record and be able to correct or reject the suggestion. An independent reviewer would sample accepted classifications. We would measure agreement and errors. Confidence scores would not substitute for source evidence.

7. Who owns national data, and how is it protected?

The contract and data-governance agreement must define controller and processor roles, ownership, residency, access, retention and exit. Required controls include role separation, encryption, tested backups and tenant-isolation checks. Sensitive information should enter only approved AI services. A sovereign-data charter states principles, but implementation and contractual terms must demonstrate them.

8. What exactly do you want from Halcyon tomorrow?

A suitable pilot sponsor, an independent climate-finance reviewer, and feedback on the first reporting workflow. We would then agree a baseline, acceptance criteria, responsibilities and a costed scope. I am not asking the audience to treat the platform as already approved by GCF or to accept untested impact claims.

9. How are ESG, GCF and SDGs different?

ESG organizes environmental, social and governance considerations. GCF applies its own investment criteria and policies to funding proposals. SDGs are global development goals. GCFT can map evidence across them while retaining each framework’s distinct requirements.

10. Does the platform issue audit or verification certificates?

It can organize review records and verification evidence. Any independent audit opinion or certification must come from a competent, authorized party under a defined standard. An internally generated certificate is not an external assurance opinion.

11. Can an immutable audit trail guarantee truthful data?

No. A protected history can show what changed and who approved it, but false source information can still be recorded. We need provenance, source validation, access controls and independent checks. Claims of immutability require technical testing and a clear threat model.

12. How will gender tagging change outcomes for women?

Tagging should lead to a substantive gender assessment, funded actions, named responsibilities and monitored access and benefits. We should examine who participates, who controls resources and whether barriers change. Counting women or adding a marker alone is insufficient.

13. Which SDG targets are most relevant?

Priority links are 13.2, 13.3 and 13.b for planning and capacity; 5.c and its allocation-tracking indicator for gender; 16.6 for accountable institutions; and 17.18 for disaggregated data. Platform indicators are not automatically the official SDG indicators. Sector goals require additional project evidence.

14. How do you distinguish adaptation from mitigation?

Mitigation requires an emissions boundary, baseline, method and appropriate factors. Adaptation requires a climate hazard, vulnerability, response logic and a measurable change in resilience. A climate label or expenditure amount demonstrates neither by itself.

15. What are the pilot size, cost and commercial model?

We propose one payer, one workflow and an agreed sample of records. We will price onboarding, methodology mapping, integration, hosting, support and any independent review after discovery. The commercial hypothesis is paid implementation plus recurring MRVaaS. This presentation does not assert a validated price or margin.

16. Why not use Excel or an existing reporting platform?

A small portfolio may be managed well in Excel. GCFT must justify its cost through linked evidence, controlled review, reuse across reports and institutional continuity. We would compare a specific workflow against the current process. If the difference does not justify the cost, we should narrow or stop the offer.

17. Are the 95% and 30% figures achieved results?

No. They are proposed pilot tests. We would define mandatory metadata and comparable reporting tasks, measure the baseline, and agree targets with the partner. A small pilot can show directional evidence but cannot establish performance across all SIDS.

18. Will GCF accept an XBRL export or every framework mapping?

The TOC proposes machine-readable and XBRL-oriented outputs. We have not established that GCF accepts a specific GCFT taxonomy or export as an official submission. We must map to the actual current templates and validate with the intended recipient. GCF criteria, OECD markers and other classifications remain separate.

19. How much climate finance or emissions reduction can you claim?

This material establishes no verified amount attributable to GCFT. We would separately track finance recorded, prepared, approved, disbursed and absorbed, with sources and dates. Physical climate outcomes require project-specific measurement and clear attribution limits.

20. Who will deliver, and what evidence would make you stop?

The founder and OECS AI Institute provide the proposed institutional base, with named software, methodology and governance partners still needed for a contracted deployment. We would pause without a payer, lawful data access, acceptable data quality or safe operation. Poor adoption or unfavorable service costs would trigger redesign.
